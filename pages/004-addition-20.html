Addition

We know that \(0 + 1 = 1\) and that \(1 + 1 = 2\).
What about \((1 + 1) + (1 + 1)\)?

This is just counting. When you add numbers, you can group them any way you like. (This is called "associativity", since you are associating the addition operation to two operands at a time. Or, you could say that associativity gives you a *choice* as to which two operands to add first.)

So for our problem of \((1 + 1) + (1 + 1)\), I can think of two ways to go about solving it.

  1. The first involves grouping the 1's in a different way. Let's say we choose to ungroup the 1's and re-group them from left to right. It would look like \(((((0 + 1) + 1) + 1) + 1)\). Then we can count up the 1's just like counting. $$\begin{aligned} ((((0 + 1) + 1) + 1) + 1) &= ((((\text{zero} + 1) + 1) + 1) + 1) \\ &= (((\text{one} + 1) + 1) + 1) \\ &= ((\text{two} + 1) + 1) \\ &= (\text{three} + 1) \\ &= \text{four} \end{aligned}$$ See that this accounts for all of the 1's and results in \((1 + 1) + (1 + 1) = 4\)
  2. The second involves the result from the first, and keeps the grouping of the 1's. $$\begin{aligned} (1 + 1) + (1 + 1) &= \text{two} + \text{two} \\ ((((0 + 1) + 1) + 1) + 1) &= \text{two} + \text{two} \\ \text{four} &= \text{two} + \text{two} \end{aligned}$$ So now you know something about multiplication too, that two, two times, equals four, or \(2 \times 2 = 4\).
